EASTWAR Devlog #046

[3D Transition Series] Designing the Soldiers for the Battlefield

October 20, 2026

13 concept images · October 20, 2026. 3D conversion and in-game use remain subjects for subsequent testing.

After the battlefield, the soldiers who will stand on it

In Dev Log 045, I explored adding a 3D view to EASTWAR without rebuilding its battle rules. Today I worked on the soldiers for that experiment: Korean, Chinese, Japanese and Mongol units that belong to the same game, yet remain distinguishable at a distance.

These are reference images for future 3D conversion, not finished, animated game assets. Making one appealing soldier is different from making several formations readable on a battlefield. This work prepares for the latter.

A shared set of promises

AI-generated images can drift between styles. One soldier may look like a stylized strategy-game unit, the next like a realistic film character. To keep the series coherent, I wrote production principles and unit guides as Markdown files.

Think of them as a shared set of promises rather than a complicated technical manual: consistent proportions, recognizable helmets and armor, clear weapons, and explicit exclusions. Each new archer or rider can be compared with the soldiers already made.

The priority is large shapes that remain readable at gameplay scale. Spears, bows and quivers, and the combination of horse and rider should identify the unit before tiny surface details do. The basic Korean, Chinese and Japanese spearmen have no shields. Commanders are planned as portraits above their formations rather than separate 3D hero models: soldiers communicate unit type, portraits communicate leadership.

These documents can change as the design develops. This entry records the direction as of October 20, 2026, including the latest decisions about unique units.

Korean units: blue regulars and a modest militia

Blue clothing, dark armor and a shared helmet design connect the Korean spearman, archer and cavalryman. The aim is family resemblance across different weapons, with cultural identity carried by silhouettes as well as color.

The planned Korean unique unit is the militia. A topknot and headband, simple clothing, straw sandals and a plain spear distinguish it from armored regulars. That contrast should survive a smaller view. Its specific combat abilities will be developed alongside the game rules later.

Japanese units: red armor and a gunner

Red and black, broad helmet shapes and a consistent armor design tie the Japanese spearman, archer and cavalryman together while distinguishing them from their neighbors.

The planned Japanese unique unit is the gunner. A long barrel and a firearm-ready stance should separate it clearly from an archer. This is a choice about faction identity in EASTWAR, not a claim about the entirety of military history.

Chinese units: green lamellar armor and a repeating crossbow

Deep green, bronze-colored trim and layered armor connect the Chinese units. The spearman establishes a visual family that continues through the archer, cavalryman and unique unit.

The planned Chinese unique unit is the repeating crossbowman. Its transverse bow, top-mounted bolt magazine and operating lever are the key shapes. The weapon must read differently from an ordinary bow at a small size, while remaining simple enough to interpret and refine during 3D conversion.

Mongol units: horse and bow as one identity

Rather than repeating the same roster, the Mongol force is being conceived around horse archers. The rider, short bow and quiver form a single readable unit. The bow replaces the spear used by the regular cavalry concepts.

Mounted units need particular care in testing. Shrinking only the horse can distort its relationship with the rider; preserving natural proportions can make a formation crowded. A convincing individual render still needs to work when several riders occupy a battlefield cell.

From reference image to an actual battlefield

Next, I plan to use tools such as Tripo AI to turn these references into candidate 3D models. I will inspect and refine their forms, materials and movement requirements in Blender, then test them in Godot.

A successful conversion is not the same as a finished game asset. Hands and weapons, horse-and-rider proportions, movement and turning all need inspection. Readability and performance with multiple formations on screen will also determine whether the approach is suitable.

Today's images are therefore a starting point for experiments, not proof that the conversion is complete. The decision to use them in the game will follow the Blender and Godot tests.

Three routes remain open

As of October 20, 2026, I am considering three approaches:

  1. A 3D battlefield with 3D units. Terrain and soldiers would both be three-dimensional.
  2. A 2D battlefield with 3D units. Preserve the current battlefield's 2.5D-like appearance while adding volume and movement to the soldiers.
  3. A 2D battlefield with 2D units. Develop the depth and visual expression of the current 2.5D-like presentation further.

My current priority is the second option: 3D units on a 2D battlefield. It is not a final decision. Perspective, scale, lighting and shadows must fit the background, and that can only be judged in context.

Testing may favor full 3D, or show that improving the existing 2D approach serves the game better. The point is to make the battlefield easier to read and better to play. Today I prepared the soldiers needed to explore that choice. Next comes seeing them on the battlefield.
