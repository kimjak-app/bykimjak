Cities Coming to Life on the World Map

Building Korean, Chinese, and Japanese City Assets, a City Development HUD, and a Day–Night Turn Button

2026.11.07

Once the cities were in place on the 3D world map, I wanted to show how they were developing inside their walls, too. Alongside locating a city on the map, I wanted players to be able to see the city they were building at a glance.

The focus of this round of work was a city development HUD window that could show exactly that. A HUD is an on-screen element that displays information you need while playing. I added a small window to EASTWAR for viewing a city’s development visually.

Looking Inside a City from the Map

Clicking a city icon opens a window showing its interior. I settled on a fixed 2.5D view that works independently of the 3D world map camera. This gives the city a sense of depth without rotating along with the map when the player moves the camera.

I decided to keep the window small and position it on the left side of the screen. Placing it over the currently empty Mongolian area is intended to let players see the map and the city view together.

I approved five sample city views, and Korean, Chinese, and Japanese building sets were placed in Hanseong, Pyongyang, Jianye, Edo, and Kyoto. The market and government-office images shown here also differ by country. These are the visual ingredients for making a city’s cultural setting recognizable when players look inside.

Replacing Artwork Without Rewriting Code

For the city HUD, I approved the recommended options for all seven decisions. An important part of this was separating building placement, anchor points, and drawing order into data.

In simple terms, we created a separate set of layout instructions for the city artwork. The structure allows building images to be replaced without rewriting the program code each time. These rules will also serve as a basis for refining the city views later.

The assets for every region are not ready yet. Karakorum still uses a temporary grid. A Mongolian building set featuring gers, palisades, and grassland remains an essential task. Connecting the city interior window to the technology tree is also included in the agreed sequence of work.

We divided the responsibilities, too. Rok Secretary handles map baking and HUD-related patches and documentation, Clcoach handles review and work instructions, and Claude Code handles implementation and F6 testing. I passed the finalized city HUD decisions directly to Clcoach.

A Small Sky Cycles When a Turn Ends

Alongside the city development HUD, I revised the end-turn button. The old compass design became a circular window onto the sky. A navy-blue cloud band sits inside a gold rim, surrounded by a ring based on the twelve traditional time divisions. 子 sits at the top and 午 at the bottom, with 子, 卯, 午, and 酉 displayed more prominently.

When a turn ends, the sky inside the button changes through sun, sunset, moon, and dawn. The cycle takes about 1–1.5 seconds, returning to daytime for the next turn. It gives the small button a sense of time passing.

The entire circular window is clickable. I removed the separate “End Turn” label panel and use a tooltip, a short explanation that appears when the mouse hovers over the button, instead.

Internally, only the appearance of the existing TurnEndCompass was changed. No new node was created, and its signals, which communicate actions such as a button click, and waiting logic were kept intact. The visual design was refined while preserving the existing behavior structure.

I also settled on a 512px image set for use in the game. According to the work log, memory use for this set fell from about 25MB to 6.3MB. These figures refer to the turn-button image set, not the game’s total memory use.

Next, Refining the Rest of the Interface

Reworking the turn button also established the next goal. I decided to refine the rest of the world map HUD, originally built during the MVP stage to check the basic functionality, to match the new button’s quality.

There are six areas to review: the top menu icons, the country panel on the left, the city preview, the city panel on the right, the city flags on the map, and the turn button. The plan is to preserve the layout and information structure while improving borders, materials, typography, and icon textures.

The agreed order is to create panel concepts first, then refine the menu icons, city-preview borders, and map flags, before compiling the instructions for Clcoach. One decision remains open: whether to give both side panels the full gold-and-navy treatment, or retain their existing dark surfaces and coordinate only the accents.

This round added a window for showing city development and brought more of EASTWAR’s visual character to the end-turn button. The remaining work is clear, including the Mongolian building set and the overall interface design. I plan to fill in those pieces one by one, refining an interface that lets players look more closely at the cities on the map.
