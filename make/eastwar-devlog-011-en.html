Dev Log · #011

The World Came Alive

Armies, Trade, and Diplomacy Enter the Game

May 13 — May 15, 2026

Three Days, One Shift

Some stretches of development pass slowly. Others, when you look back, have changed almost everything. May 13 through 15 was the second kind. Armies were restructured. Supply lines were connected. Trade began. The world map expanded to thirteen cities. And the stage of war stretched from Korea, China, and Japan all the way to the Mongolian steppe.

Too much happened to summarize in one sentence. So I'll go in order.

Armies, Split Three Ways

v0.5-4 ~ v0.5-4c — 2026.05.13

Until now, "troops" in EASTWAR was a single number. The figure a hero carried. It handled combat, maintained city order, defended walls, and ate up maintenance costs — all in one bucket.

That changed. Armies were split into three distinct categories.

First, hero troops — combat units led directly into battle by a hero. Second, garrison troops — soldiers permanently stationed in a city to maintain public order and defend against attack. Third, recruitable troops — civilians not yet in uniform, waiting to be drafted.

Separating these wasn't a small edit. It meant redesigning population-to-garrison ratios, military overpopulation penalties, and maintenance cost breakdowns from scratch. A new rule came with it: if garrison troops exceed 50% of total city population, the city suffers a loyalty penalty on the following turn.

For the first time, armies in EASTWAR have structure, not just numbers.

Heroes Take Their Own Soldiers

v0.5-5 ~ v0.5-5b — 2026.05.13

With armies restructured, the next question was how they'd be deployed.

Heroes now have troop caps tied to their rank. Governors lead up to 10,000. Generals lead up to 8,000. Vice-commanders up to 6,000. Military officers up to 5,000. When a hero marches to war, they draw troops from the city garrison and lead them out.

Post-battle resolution became realistic too. A victory returns surviving soldiers to the city, with 30% of casualties treated as wounded rather than dead. A defeat leaves half of all deployed troops as wounded. Wounded soldiers recover over three turns and return to the garrison.

A single battle now requires a full accounting.

Factions Get Names

v0.5-6 — 2026.05.13

Until now, EASTWAR only had two sides: the player and everyone else.

That's no longer true. Each group now has its own faction identity — Luoyang, Hanseong, Pyeongyang, Kyoto, and more. Enemy factions can now appoint their own chancellors and governors. Before, only the player could do that.

This was also a structural prerequisite. Without named factions with persistent identities, trade and multi-city conquest couldn't be built on top. It looks like renaming on the surface. In practice, it's the skeleton of a real strategy game.

Internal Supply Lines First

v0.5-7 ~ v0.5-7c — 2026.05.14

Before external trade, the game needed to handle logistics inside a single faction.

The concept is simple. Hanseong is a rear city. Pyeongyang is a border city. If Hanseong has surplus troops and Pyeongyang is short — the system should recognize that and act.

v0.5-7 built the judgment layer. Cities were classified by role — rear, border, frontline. Troop surpluses and shortfalls were calculated and displayed. But the troops themselves didn't move yet.

v0.5-7c made them move. Surplus troops flow from rear cities to underfunded frontline cities. Three rules constrain it: no city drops below its minimum garrison, movement is capped per turn, and the chancellor takes priority over governors in deciding what to send where. Internal logistics now runs on its own.

Then Comes Trade Between Factions

v0.5-8 ~ v0.5-8c — 2026.05.14

With internal supply sorted, the next question followed naturally: what about other factions?

External trade came with a hard rule. Any two factions that have fought go into a 10-turn trade suspension. No commerce with enemies — an obvious principle that felt surprisingly real once it was in the code. When the cooldown ends, relations reset to neutral and trade resumes.

The substance of trade followed: what gets exchanged, and how much. Chancellors run automated optimal trade. Governors handle a more limited range. Players can intervene directly via a trade adjustment panel. Gold, food, salt, and specialty goods now flow between factions in real time.

Four Cities Became Thirteen

v0.5-8e ~ v0.5-8k — 2026.05.14 ~ 15

Once the structure is solid, expansion is fast. Cities grew from 4 to 10, then to 13. Heroes expanded from 8 to 27. Ten factions now populate the world.

Baekje joined the Korean peninsula — Uija of Baekje, Gyebaek, and Heukchi Sangji. Kyushu emerged in southwestern Japan — Shimazu Yoshihiro and Konishi Yukinaga. On May 15, the Mongolian steppe opened up: Karakorum appeared on the world map.

City coordinate placement was rebuilt at the same time. A percentage-based coordinate system replaced the old fixed-pixel approach. Now no matter what screen resolution a player uses, every city appears in exactly the right place on the map.

The Battlefield Got Sharper

v0.5-9 ~ v0.5-9c — 2026.05.15

The battle screen moved from Phaser.js rendering to a DOM-based overlay. The reason was simple: clarity. Text and numbers on the old Phaser canvas looked blurry. The DOM version renders them crisp.

Unit movement became smooth. Troop formations now glide to their target tiles with tween animation instead of jumping. The abrupt flash when entering battle was fixed — background, hero portraits, and troop labels now appear together as one clean fade-in.

Allied and enemy units now face each other. Direction indicators moved close to their units. For the first time, the battle screen actually looked like a game.

Diplomacy and Espionage Join the World

v0.5-10 — 2026.05.15

The last addition of these three days was a scaffold for diplomacy and espionage. Not the full system — just the skeleton. Faction relationship states defined. Espionage actions outlined. Empty hooks waiting for intelligence missions, negotiation flows, and treaty systems to be built on top.

Looking back at May 13 through 15, one thing stands out: EASTWAR started to feel like a strategy game. The battles were always there. Now there's statecraft behind them — economies to manage, supply lines to protect, relationships to cultivate or betray. Historical figures from across East Asia are finally moving through a world that feels like theirs.
