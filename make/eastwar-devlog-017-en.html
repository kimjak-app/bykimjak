EASTWAR Devlog #017

World Map Ported In & the Camera Follows the Battlefield

May 27, 2026

Battle Gets More Intense, and the World Starts Taking Shape

These two days split into two directions: making the current battle feel more intense, and starting to draw the actual world this battle takes place in — the world map. They look like completely different tasks, but they're aimed at the same goal: moving from "battle demo" to "strategy game."

The Camera Starts Following the Battle

Until now, the battle camera stayed fixed no matter where units were on screen. This time, I gave it life: selecting a unit, moving, attacking, using a unique skill, or a reinforcement arriving now all pull the camera smoothly toward the action.

But moving the camera brought an unexpected side effect. As the world scrolled under the camera, things like facing arrows and post-move direction buttons would briefly appear detached from the unit's actual position. The camera moves the world, while UI elements are supposed to stay fixed on screen — and the two were using different position calculations. Syncing their timing took a few more passes.

Unique Skills Now Actually Land With a Punch

When I first added the unique skill system, activating one felt like just a big popup appearing. This time I gave it real impact — a brief screen shake, an ink-burst impact effect, and timing tightened to match the short rhythm of a turn-change toast. The goal was to make it feel less like "a notice pops up and fades," and more like "a hit lands and it's gone."

Finally, a Map Exists

And the real highlight of these two days: for the first time, an actual world map image entered the project. It's split into four tiles stitched together — a setup that will make it much easier to refine or swap out individual regions later.

Placing four images on screen wasn't the whole job — the scene structure had to be set up properly from the start too: a layer for map tiles, one reserved for future roads, one for cities, one for moving armies, one for atmospheric effects like clouds and flags. Splitting things into layers by role now means anything added later won't tangle with what's already there.

Cities Take Their Place on the Map

With the map in place, it was time to put cities on it. Luoyang, Pyeongyang, Hanseong, Gyeongju, Sabi, Yecheng, Chengdu, Jianye, Kyoto, Osaka, Kyushu, Edo — I placed markers one by one, based on city data already defined in the web version.

I locked in one principle here: the web version's coordinates are just a rough starting draft — "put it around here" — while the final position is whatever I drag it to in the editor by hand. At first, the map tiles and city markers were using different coordinate systems, leading to the slightly comic situation of cities floating in the gray area outside the map. I unified that. The same principle now applies to the four map tiles themselves — code no longer auto-calculates their position; whatever I set by hand in the editor is respected as-is. I also fixed city name labels not moving together with their icons.

What's Next

With the map and cities in place, next comes getting armies to actually move between those cities, and connecting this world map to the battle screen the moment a fight breaks out.
